# Legal

The two pages a published site owes, and the rule that keeps them from being
invented.

## Why this is not the fabrication rule's problem

`build/SKILL.md` says an invented metric and a fabricated testimonial are one
failure, and a build following it correctly deleted a footer's terms and privacy
links on the grounds that the pages did not exist. That was the right reading of
the rule and the wrong outcome, and the difference is worth stating precisely,
because the rule itself is not changing.

A testimonial is a claim about a person who did not say it. A metric is a claim
about a result nobody measured. Neither is owed by anyone, and the honest move is
silence. A privacy policy is not a claim about the world — it is a disclosure of
something the site is already doing, and the site does it whether or not the page
exists. Deleting the link does not remove the obligation. It removes the notice
and keeps the collection, which is the only outcome of the three that is
unlawful.

So the amendment is narrow. **A legal page is written from what the build already
knows, and a field nobody answered ships as a visible gap rather than as an
invention.** The fabrication rule is untouched: nothing here licenses inventing a
company name, a jurisdiction, a retention period, or a processor. It licenses
writing down the ones that are known and marking the ones that are not.

**None of this is legal advice, and the file says so out loud in the artifact.**
What the build produces is a draft that names, accurately, what the site does. A
lawyer decides whether that draft is sufficient in the jurisdiction it will be
read in. Say that in the handover, not only in the page.

## The inventory this draws on

Root 1 collects what the product is. These are the questions that root does not
ask and this page cannot be written without. Ask them where the brief is silent;
they are cheap, and every one of them has a real answer the person requesting the
build already knows.

**Who is publishing** — the legal entity, its registration number where it has
one, and the country whose law governs. This is the field most often absent, and
it is the one that decides which of the two regimes below applies.

**A contact route** for a privacy request. An address that reaches a person.

**What is collected, and by what.** Read it off the tree rather than asking: a
form field is collection, an analytics script is collection, a session cookie is
collection, an embedded video is collection by a third party, a font served from
someone else's domain is an IP address handed to them. The build knows this
because the build wrote it.

**Why each thing is collected**, in one clause per item. This is the field that
turns a template into a disclosure.

**Who else receives it** — every processor the tree actually calls: the host, the
analytics vendor, the payment processor, the mailing list, the error tracker.

**How long it is kept**, where the product knows.

**Whether anything is sold or used to train anything.** If the answer is no, say
no. It is the sentence readers look for.

## The rule for an unanswered field

Where an answer does not exist, the page ships the field with the gap visible —
in the rendered page, not in a code comment — and the handover names every gap in
one list. A bracketed placeholder in the published copy is exactly the surviving
placeholder F12 fires on, so the marker has to read as a deliberate hole rather
than as text nobody replaced: a short line saying which fact is missing and that
the page is incomplete without it.

An unanswered field is never filled by analogy with another product. "Most SaaS
keep logs for 90 days" is an invented retention period, and it is the same
failure as an invented testimonial with worse consequences, because someone may
rely on it.

## What a privacy notice contains

The order below is the reader's, not the statute's. A policy organised by legal
article is a policy nobody finishes.

What is collected, item by item, in the reader's words rather than the
mechanism's — *the email you type into the form*, not *user_email* · why each
item is collected · who else receives it, named · how long each is kept · what
the reader can do about it, and how · who to contact · when the notice last
changed.

**Under the LGPD** (Brazil, Lei 13.709/2018) add: the legal basis for each
purpose, from the article 7 list, rather than a general statement of consent ·
the rights in article 18 named as things the reader can request — confirmation,
access, correction, anonymisation, portability, deletion, information about
sharing, and revocation of consent · the controller's identity, and the data
protection officer's contact where the operation requires one.

**Under the GDPR** (the EU, and anything reaching it) add: the legal basis per
purpose · the article 15–22 rights · any transfer outside the EEA and the
mechanism relied on · the right to complain to a supervisory authority.

Where the publisher and the audience sit in different regimes, the page covers
both rather than choosing. This is a real cost in length and it is not
negotiable.

## What terms contain

Terms are lighter and more product-shaped, so most of this is derived rather than
required: who may use the service and from what age · what the person may and may
not do with it · who owns what — the product's material, and the user's ·
whether there is a fee, what it buys, and how it ends · what happens to an
account that is closed, by either side · the disclaimers and the limit of
liability, which is the clause the lawyer will rewrite · the governing law, which
is the jurisdiction from the inventory · how a change to the terms is announced.

## Cookies and consent

Only where there is something to consent to. A site with a session cookie and
nothing else does not need a banner, and shipping one anyway is the same
unthinking template as any other.

A banner is owed where non-essential cookies exist — analytics, advertising,
anything third-party. Where it is owed: nothing non-essential fires before the
answer, refusing is exactly as easy as accepting, and the choice can be changed
later from somewhere the reader can find. A banner whose only button is *Accept*
records nothing, because a consent that could not be refused was never given.

## Routing

The links go in the footer, and they go there **only once the pages exist**. This
is the half of the original decision that was right and stays: a footer column
whose links 404 is worse than no footer column.

So the order is fixed. The pages are written, then the links are added, and a
build that cannot write the pages — because the inventory came back empty and the
person requesting it is unreachable — reports that as the finding it is rather
than shipping either a dead link or a fiction.

Where a form collects anything, the notice is also linked from the form itself,
beside the submit control, not only from the footer.
