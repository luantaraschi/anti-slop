# Template

Did the content choose this shape, or did the mould?

Nine tells on structure. These are the hardest to see while reading and the
easiest to see while counting, so count: items per list, sentences per
paragraph, paragraphs per section. A single instance of anything here is
nothing. The tell is the repetition, which is why almost every `Signal` on this
axis names a count and what the count decides.

### T1 — Three of everything

**Signal**  Three items in a list, three examples per claim, three clauses in a sentence, and the same three across sections. Count the enumerations in the text: the tell fires when three is the most common count and no section departs from it.

**Principle**  Three is the count a generator reaches for when nobody counted. Its repetition across a whole text is what gives it away, not any single triple.

**Fix**  Count what actually exists and write that number. Two if there are two. Five if there are five. Where a real triple survives, leave it.

**Not slop when**  the counts vary across the text, or the three are named by the source, or the genre fixes the number, as in a three act structure or a legal test with three prongs.

### T2 — The negative parallelism

**Signal**  A claim built by first denying a lesser version of itself: *not only X but also Y*, *it is not just about X, it is Y*, *this is not X, it is Y*. The clipped form is the same move compressed: a tailing fragment such as *no guesswork*, *no wasted motion*, *no surprises* stuck onto the end of a sentence.

**Principle**  The denied half is usually a claim nobody made. The construction manufactures contrast and delivers one idea while sounding like two.

**Fix**  Write the positive claim on its own. Where the contrast is real, name what actually holds the opposite view.

**Not slop when**  the denied half is a position the text has already attributed to someone, so the contrast has a subject.

### T3 — A range whose ends are not on one scale

**Signal**  *From X to Y* where X and Y are not endpoints of anything: *from startups to enterprises*, *from the Big Bang to dark matter*, *from onboarding to insight*.

**Principle**  The construction promises a spectrum and delivers two examples. The reader tries to place the middle and finds there is none.

**Fix**  List the examples as examples, or name the real dimension and its real ends.

**Not slop when**  the two ends are genuinely the extremes of a stated scale, as in a date range, a price band or a severity ladder.

### T4 — The bolded header list

**Signal**  A vertical list where every item opens with a bolded phrase and a colon, and the text after the colon restates the phrase: *Performance: performance has been improved*.

**Principle**  The bolded stub is a heading for a paragraph that never arrives. It makes a list look organised while carrying one sentence per item.

**Fix**  Where the items are related, write them as prose. Where the list is right, cut the stubs and let the items say themselves.

**Not slop when**  the stubs are genuine terms being defined, as in a glossary, a parameter table or an API reference, and the text after each is not a restatement.

### T5 — A heading that restates itself before starting

**Signal**  A heading, then a one line paragraph that says the heading again in a sentence, then the real content: *## Performance* followed by *Speed matters.*

**Principle**  A rhetorical warm up. It reads as padding on the second occurrence and as a habit by the fourth.

**Fix**  Delete the warm up line. The heading already did that work.

**Not slop when**  the line states something the heading does not, such as the scope of the section or a definition the rest depends on.

### T6 — The challenges and prospects section

**Signal**  A section, usually near the end, that concedes difficulties in the abstract and then recovers: *despite these challenges*, *challenges and opportunities*, *looking ahead*, *areas for improvement*. The difficulties named are the ones any subject of that kind would have.

**Principle**  A generator writes this section because texts of this shape have one, not because the subject presented a difficulty. It is the essay equivalent of a placeholder.

**Fix**  Keep only the difficulties the source actually names, and put each one where it belongs in the text rather than in a pen at the end.

**Not slop when**  the difficulties are specific, sourced, and would not apply to a neighbouring subject.

### T7 — Announcing the writing instead of writing

**Signal**  Meta commentary about what the text is about to do: *let us dive in*, *let us explore*, *here is what you need to know*, *in this section we will*, *before we begin*, *without further ado*.

**Principle**  The announcement costs a sentence and delivers none. It is the tutorial script's reflex, and it slows every paragraph it opens.

**Fix**  Delete it and start. Where a text genuinely needs a map, one sentence naming what is in it beats one sentence saying it is about to begin.

**Not slop when**  the document is a spoken script or a course, where the signpost is doing work the page layout cannot do.

### T8 — Staccato drama

**Signal**  A run of short declarative fragments used to build weight: three or more in sequence, each landing like a closer, often stacking negations. *It had no preference. No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia.*

**Principle**  One short sentence for emphasis is a tool. A run of them is a rhythm nobody chose, and it inflates the tone above what the content supports.

**Fix**  Fold the run into one or two sentences that state the claim, and keep at most one short sentence for the landing.

**Not slop when**  the register is genuinely dramatic by the author's habit and the text also contains long sentences, so the short ones are a contrast rather than the baseline.

### T9 — One measure for every paragraph

**Signal**  Paragraphs of near identical length throughout, sentences clustered in one mid range, sections carrying the same number of subsections. Measure it: count sentences per paragraph across the text and look at the spread.

**Principle**  Real writing is uneven, because some things need more room than others. A uniform measure means the shape was decided before the content was.

**Fix**  Let the important parts run long and compress the dull ones. Merge and split paragraphs freely. Depth does not have to be uniform, and making it uniform is what flattened it.

**Not slop when**  the format fixes the measure, as in release notes, an entry list, a form or a table, or the text is short enough that the spread cannot be measured.
